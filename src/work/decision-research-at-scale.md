## Paven: financial wellbeing for Meta employees

### The problem

Meta asked Round Feather why employees weren't using the benefits and perks already available to them. Earlier exploratory research by the team had found four reasons:

- Benefits were spread across disconnected providers.
- Employees had knowledge gaps and little personalised guidance.
- Confusing terminology and low awareness kept people away.
- Financial stress weighed on people in a high-performance workplace.

The answer was Paven, an app that guides employees toward their financial goals and connects them to the benefits they already had. My job was to keep testing it against employees' needs as it was designed and built.

{% figure "paven-app-screens", "Three Paven app screens on phones: a home screen with next steps and recommended articles, a grid of benefit collections such as coaching, tax resources and retirement savings, and a screen for contacting a financial coach.", "Paven: personalised next steps, benefit collections, and direct access to a financial coach.", 800, 450 %}

### Three phases of testing

1. **Concept.** In my first week, I helped introduce the Paven proof of concept to Meta's early adopters in Menlo Park. The prototype had fixed flows, so I ran scripted, guided walkthroughs built around three research-informed personas, with planned pauses and prompts for feedback. More than a dozen employees took part, and I presented the results to Meta's benefits leadership.
2. **First release.** Over three months I tested 15+ features and their task flows with Meta employees, one feature a week. Each week ran the full cycle: align on the feature, plan, test, analyse and hand insights to design and development.
3. **After launch.** Analytics showed retention problems: push notifications and the home page drove most engagement, and other features saw little use. Across seven initiatives, I combined secondary research, heuristic evaluations and user testing to find out why.

### Outcome

Since launch, Paven has onboarded 14,176 users. 83% of them set financial goals in the app, and it initiated 7,554 connections to benefits. 44% of tax advising appointments and 58% of financial coaching engagements happened through Paven. Testing features before they were built also saved redesign work, which the team estimated at 22% of the cost of each sprint.

## A digital platform for people facing an unexpected pregnancy

### The problem

A New York non-profit supported people facing an unexpected pregnancy through a high-touch, in-person model. It could not reach everyone who needed it, and its website offered little information, little interaction and no structure to help people decide while protecting their privacy. The research question: how might digital care reduce stress, close knowledge gaps and protect privacy, while supporting informed, autonomous decisions?

I led the research in a team with a UX designer and a content designer, working with the client's sponsor and three advisers in wellbeing and healthcare.

### Four concepts, tested with the people they were for

Drawing on our earlier research with the client, we prototyped four concepts:

- **Journaling:** guided self-reflection to process emotions, with prompts, emotion check-ins and short meditations.
- **Discovery:** a personalised reading plan to learn about the options available.
- **Crisis messaging:** live support from an advocate in the first, most overwhelming moments.
- **Community:** stories from others who had been through it, and spaces to share.

{% figure "pregnancy-platform-journaling-plan", "A journaling plan screen with a welcome message, three cards labelled Personalized, Open-ended and Transformational, and a five-day plan starting with Day 1, Identify your emotions.", "The journaling concept: a personalised five-day plan.", 800, 756 %}

I ran testing sessions with 20 participants, 10 women and 10 men of diverse ages, ethnicities and experiences. Some had been through the experience themselves, others as partners. Sessions combined task-based activities, guided walkthroughs and follow-up conversations.

{% figure "pregnancy-platform-emotion-check-in", "A check-in screen asking 'How do you feel after the session today?', with eight illustrated options such as more empowered, more calm and more hopeful.", "A post-session emotion check-in from the journaling prototype.", 800, 473 %}

### What we learned

The central tension was how to create safe connection without compromising privacy, and how to be emotionally honest without overwhelming people. The sessions turned assumptions into design directions:

- **Men were underserved.** There was a large gap in resources for partners.
- **Tone is a safety issue.** Content had to be warm and non-judgmental, but never too upbeat or too branded.
- **People need choices in format.** We recommended audio, transcript, chat and visual versions of content.
- **Emotional safety is a feature.** It had to be designed in, not left as a by-product.

These findings changed the content structure, onboarding flow, visual identity and moderation rules. The team prioritised journaling and discovery, the two concepts that met participants' needs best. I then worked with the developers so the launched product kept the relevance, tone and sense of safety the research called for.

### Outcome

By the first quarter of 2024, the platform had reached 35,000+ users since its 2023 launch. More than 10,000 had used its interactive features, and 3,500+ had created an account and completed at least one journaling plan or reading list.
